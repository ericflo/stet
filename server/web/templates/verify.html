Verify scores with your GPU

Every number on the leaderboard is measured on a verifier's machine: the organisers' own, and volunteers' like yours. A verifier is handed an edit script, which is plain data, trains the fixed model on it and reports how it does on the round's exam. Nobody else's code runs on your machine. One command, and you can leave it running.

Do this

  1. Install

    It brings its own Python and the build of PyTorch for your GPU.

    {{template "install" .}}

  2. Check the machine

    Says whether the GPU, the libraries and the disk are what grading needs.

    $ stet doctor

  3. Make an account

    A key on your disk and a public name. No email, no password.

    $ stet login --handle yourname

  4. Start verifying

    It waits for a round, does the work it is handed, and waits again. Ctrl-C stops it and hands back whatever it held.

    $ stet verify

  5. Leave it running

    On Linux this makes it a service: it starts with the machine, comes back if it stops, and upgrades itself between jobs when a new version is out.

    $ stet service install

What happens next

You do nothing. This is what the machine does.

  1. when a round is drawn

    It builds the round for itself

    It fetches the round's raw pages from Common Crawl, about a gigabyte, and checks the draw against the public randomness beacon. If it is asked to, it also trains the model once on the unedited pages: {{with .Season.Baselines.PerRound}}{{.}} {{end}}established machines a round are asked, and every machine that is not yet established. The mean of what the established ones measure is the baseline every score is measured from.

  2. ten to twenty minutes each

    It grades the scripts it is handed

    One training run per script: about ten minutes on a fast GPU, nearer twenty on a mid-range one. You cannot choose which, and you are never handed your own entry's.

  3. your first {{$d.Establish}} {{plural $d.Establish "grade" "grades"}}

    You are checked against others

    At first your grades are replications: compared with established verifiers' and published, deciding nothing. After {{$d.Establish}} that {{plural $d.Establish "agrees" "agree"}}{{if .Github}}, with GitHub linked,{{end}} you are established, and your grades make scores.

  4. when the round settles

    Your work is published

    The round's page lists every grade: who, on what GPU, what they measured. You appear on the verifiers board with the GPU time you have given, and your own entries are drawn ahead of other people's.

Questions verifiers ask

how scores are checked, in full →
Does it keep itself up to date?

Yes. When a new version is out, a verifier that holds no job installs it and starts again. It runs the same installer you ran, from this site, and nothing else. Turn it off with stet verify --no-upgrade or STET_AUTO_UPGRADE=0, and upgrade when you choose with stet upgrade.

What if my number differs from everyone else's?

Two honest machines agree to within a small tolerance. A grade outside it is published as differing and counts as a strike; after three, and more than one for every ten that agreed, the account gets no work for two weeks and may then try again. If your copy of the round itself differs, that is a broken download, and it does not count against you.

Can I stop and start whenever I like?

Yes. Ctrl-C, or stopping the service, hands back the job in hand and someone else is given it. Nothing is held against you for stopping.

I have two GPUs.

Run one verifier per GPU, each with an account of its own: set STET_CONFIG to another directory and run stet login there.

What does my machine send, and what is public?

The scores it measured on every item of the exam, hashes of the round it built, how long the work took, and a description of the machine: GPU, operating system, versions. Your handle, GPU name, grades and GPU hours are published. Your IP address is used for rate limits and is not published.

Does it trust the server?

Not for what a round is. It recomputes the round's seed from the beacon, fetches the pages from Common Crawl itself, checks the exam by hash, and refuses a round that does not match what was announced before the draw.

Can I do more?

Yes: once you are established, the same machine can run entrants' curators, which is the work the competition is shortest of.
